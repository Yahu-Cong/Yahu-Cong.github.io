CS280 Project 4A: Image Warping and Mosaicing

Overview

Photos taken from one spot with the camera rotated are related by homographies. This part recovers homographies from clicked correspondences, warps images with inverse warping (nearest neighbor and bilinear, written from scratch), and blends the warped images into mosaics, including a cylindrical projection. No cv2.warpPerspective, skimage.transform.warp or similar functions are used; only NumPy linear algebra and indexing.

A.1: Shoot the Pictures

Both sets were shot with an iPhone 17 (main camera, 26 mm 35mm-equivalent focal length), standing in one place and rotating the camera, with roughly half of each frame overlapping the next. Photos were downscaled to 1600x1200 before processing.

A.2: Recover Homographies

computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts) takes n-by-2 arrays of (x, y) correspondences and solves for the 8 unknowns of H (H22 = 1) by least squares. Each correspondence (x, y) → (x', y') gives two linear equations: 

H00*x + H01*y + H02 - H20*x*x' - H21*y*x' = x'
H10*x + H11*y + H12 - H20*x*y' - H21*y*y' = y'

Stacking n points gives A h = b with A of size 2n x 8, h = (H00, H01, H02, H10, H11, H12, H20, H21), and b = (x'1, y'1, ..., x'n, y'n). Set 1 uses 8 correspondences (16 equations), set 2 uses 6 (12 equations), so both systems are overdetermined and solved with np.linalg.lstsq.

A.3: Warp the Images

Both warpImageNearestNeighbor(im, H) and warpImageBilinear(im, H) use inverse warping: every output pixel (x, y) is mapped through H-1 to a source location; nearest neighbor rounds to the closest pixel, bilinear blends the four surrounding pixels by distance. Output pixels whose source falls outside the image stay black (the mosaics use a feathering mask as the alpha). The output box comes from pushing the four image corners through H. The per-pixel loops are vectorized with NumPy.

Rectification

Two rectangular objects in img1 were rectified: the four corners are clicked and mapped to a rectangle with the aspect ratio measured from the clicked corners. The output is cropped around the rectangle because the whole image warped by this H would be huge.

A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

Procedure: (1) compute H from the correspondences; (2) pick the reference image and warp the other into its frame (for the fixed image H is the identity); (3) the canvas is the bounding box of the reference image and the warped corners of the other image; (4) warp each image and a feathering weight mask onto the canvas with bilinear inverse warping; (5) blend with (w1*im1 + w2*im2) / (w1 + w2). The weight is 1 at the image center and falls off linearly to 0 at the borders (make_mask), so each image fades out near its own edge.

A.5: Bells & Whistles: Cylindrical Mosaics

A planar mosaic stretches the image the farther it gets from the reference image. Here the reference image's plane is wrapped onto a cylinder. For each output pixel (u, v) the cylinder angle and height are theta = (u - u0)/f, h = (v - v0)/f; the 3D point (sin theta, h, cos theta) is projected to the reference plane, x = f*sin(theta)/cos(theta) + cx, y = f*h/cos(theta) + cy, then pushed through the inverse homography into the source image and sampled bilinearly (so resolution comes from the original photos, not a resampled planar mosaic). The focal length is taken from the photo metadata: 26 mm in 35mm terms over a 36 mm frame width gives f = 26/36 * 1600 ≈ 1156 px.
